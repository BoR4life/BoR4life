# Questions — SAMPLE-rhythm-00

**Worked example, built from a fabricated case. Held for clinical review.**

Strands present in this case: **rhythm, assessment, pathology.**
Strand deliberately not covered: **imaging** — no film was taken. A
manufactured imaging question would teach the wrong thing about what was
actually available at 3am in a unit with no radiographer on site.

Five questions. Four options each. About twelve minutes.

---

### Q1 — rhythm · decision: rate control versus cardioversion in instability

A 68-year-old woman is in atrial fibrillation at 168. Her blood pressure is
88/54, she is pale and clammy, and she is telling you she feels like she is
going to pass out. The cardiology registrar asks you to draw up metoprolol
2.5 mg IV.

**What do you do?**

- A. Draw it up and give it — rate control treats the arrhythmia driving the
  hypotension
- B. Draw it up, and say that the blood pressure and her appearance make this
  an unstable rhythm; ask whether we should be preparing to cardiovert ✓
- C. Decline to give it and call a MET
- D. Give half the dose to protect the blood pressure

**Model answer — B.**

*Why B is right.* Instability moves this out of the rate-control pathway
altogether. A beta blocker in a patient who is already hypotensive removes
what remaining rate-dependent output she has, and the fall from 88/54 to
72/40 in this case is what that looks like. The nurse is not overruling the
registrar; she is naming the finding that changes the algorithm and asking
the question that follows from it.

*Why A is the attractive wrong answer.* It is attractive because it is
correct medicine for the stable patient, and because rate control is what AF
usually needs. The error is not the drug, it is the failure to check the
patient against the pathway before giving it.

*On shift.* Before you give a rate-controlling drug in fast AF, say the blood
pressure and the perfusion out loud — that one sentence is what moves the
conversation to cardioversion.

### Q2 — rhythm · decision: preparing for synchronised cardioversion

The decision is made to cardiovert at 150 J. You are setting up the
defibrillator.

**What do you check first?**

- A. That the pads are on anterior-lateral rather than anterior-posterior
- B. That SYNC is selected and the machine is marking each R wave ✓
- C. That the energy is set to biphasic maximum in case the first shock fails
- D. That suction is switched on

**Model answer — B.**

*Why B is right.* An unsynchronised shock delivered on a T wave can induce
ventricular fibrillation. Confirming sync mode and seeing the markers land on
the R waves is the check that separates cardioversion from defibrillation,
and it has to be repeated before every shock — many machines revert to
unsynchronised after each one.

*Why D is the attractive wrong answer.* Suction genuinely matters, because
this patient is about to be sedated. It is a preparation step, not the check
that makes the shock safe, and the question asks what you check first.

*On shift.* Say "sync is on, markers on the R waves" aloud before each shock,
and re-check it after every one.

### Q3 — assessment · decision: escalating on appearance before numbers

She arrives triaged as chest pain. Her observations are HR 168, BP 88/54, RR
24, SpO2 94% on room air. What actually prompted the move to a resuscitation
bay was that she looked grey.

**Which of these best describes what you do with that observation?**

- A. Record it in the nursing notes as a subjective finding and continue
  monitoring
- B. Wait for the repeat set in fifteen minutes so there is a trend to
  escalate on
- C. Escalate now, and say explicitly that the appearance is the finding —
  "she is 168 and hypotensive and she looks grey" ✓
- D. Increase the observation frequency and inform the team leader at handover

**Model answer — C.**

*Why C is right.* Appearance is a clinical finding, not a feeling, and in
this case it preceded the measured deterioration. The value of saying it out
loud is that it converts something the bedside nurse can see into something
the team can act on. Escalation that names the finding gets a different
response from escalation that reports a number.

*Why B is the attractive wrong answer.* Waiting for a trend is the right
instinct in a stable patient and the wrong one here. A patient who is
hypotensive and poorly perfused now does not become more escalatable in
fifteen minutes; she becomes less salvageable.

*On shift.* When you escalate, lead with the observation that worried you,
not the observation set.

### Q4 — pathology · decision: acting on electrolytes in an arrhythmia

Her bloods come back: potassium 3.1 mmol/L, magnesium 0.62 mmol/L, TSH
normal, troponin 46 rising to 51 ng/L.

**Which of these do you raise first, and why?**

- A. The troponin, because a rise of 5 confirms myocardial injury needing
  cardiology review
- B. The magnesium and potassium together, because both are low and both
  lower the threshold for the arrhythmia to recur after cardioversion ✓
- C. The potassium alone, because it is the electrolyte with the strongest
  effect on cardiac conduction
- D. None — treat the rhythm first and review the bloods once she is in sinus

**Model answer — B.**

*Why B is right.* Restoring sinus rhythm in a patient whose potassium and
magnesium are both low buys a rhythm that is likely to be lost again.
Magnesium is easy to overlook because it is not on every routine panel, and
replacing potassium without it is often the reason replacement appears not to
work. Raising them together is the point.

*Why A is the attractive wrong answer.* The troponin is abnormal and rising,
and it will need explaining. But a modest rise in a patient who has been at
168 with a blood pressure of 88/54 is expected demand ischaemia, and it does
not change what you do in the next ten minutes. The electrolytes do.

*On shift.* When you chase a potassium, chase the magnesium in the same
breath.

### Q5 — assessment · decision: disagreeing usefully

The registrar has asked for the metoprolol. You think the blood pressure
makes that the wrong first move. He is busy, senior to you, and has not
looked up from the chart.

**What do you say?**

- A. "Are you sure about the metoprolol?"
- B. Draw it up, give it, and document that you raised a concern
- C. "I'm worried — her BP is 88 over 54 and she looks grey. Can we talk about
  cardioverting instead?" ✓
- D. Say nothing to him and call the MET so a second opinion arrives

**Model answer — C.**

*Why C is right.* It names the concern, gives the two findings that support
it, and proposes the alternative — the structure most graded-assertiveness
frameworks teach, and the version most likely to be heard by someone who is
task-focused. The word "worried" is doing real work: it is a recognised
trigger phrase in most escalation policies.

*Why A is the attractive wrong answer.* It is what most of us actually say,
and it is the weakest available move. A closed question invites "yes", and
"yes" ends the conversation without either party having exchanged any
information.

*On shift.* Concern, evidence, proposal — in that order, in one sentence.
