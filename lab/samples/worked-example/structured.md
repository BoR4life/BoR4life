## Presenting complaint and brief history

A 68-year-old woman presented overnight in late winter with two hours of
chest tightness and a sensation she described as fluttering. No radiation, no
preceding exertion. Her husband brought her in. She had been taking
amiodarone prescribed by her general practitioner and had stopped it about
three weeks earlier; the reason for stopping was not supplied.

## Observations on arrival

- HR: 168, irregular
- BP: 88/54
- RR: 24
- SpO2: 94% on room air
- Temp: afebrile
- GCS: [NOT SUPPLIED]
- Other: pale and clammy on arrival, which prompted transfer to a
  resuscitation bay

## Investigations available

ECG · bloods

- ECG — narrow complex, irregularly irregular, no discernible P waves.
  Read as atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response.
- Bloods — potassium 3.1 mmol/L, magnesium 0.62 mmol/L, TSH within normal
  limits, troponin 46 then 51 ng/L
- ABG/VBG — not performed, or not supplied
- Echo — not performed, or not supplied

## What the team initially thought

Fast atrial fibrillation requiring rate control. The cardiology registrar
asked for intravenous metoprolol. The senior nurse disagreed and argued for
electrical cardioversion on the basis of the blood pressure.

## What it actually was

Atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response in a haemodynamically
unstable patient — that is, an unstable tachyarrhythmia rather than a rate
control problem. The recent cessation of amiodarone and the low potassium and
magnesium were contributing.

## Course and outcome

Metoprolol 2.5 mg was given intravenously. The blood pressure fell to 72/40.
The team moved to synchronised cardioversion at 150 J, which restored sinus
rhythm at 78 with recovery of the blood pressure. Electrolytes were replaced.
She went home on day two.

[AMBIGUOUS: the submission does not say whether potassium and magnesium were
replaced before or after cardioversion. The sequence matters to the teaching
and should be confirmed with the contributor before publication.]

## Teaching point

Haemodynamic instability changes the algorithm, and the nurse in the room is
often the person best placed to say so. Rate control in a patient who is
already hypotensive is a decision, not a default. The disagreement in this
case was the thing that went right.
