## The case

She was 68, and she came in overnight in late winter with two hours of chest
tightness and a feeling she described as fluttering. Her husband brought her.
Nothing about the history was dramatic — no radiation, no exertion, no
collapse.

Her observations were: heart rate 168 and irregular, blood pressure 88/54,
respiratory rate 24, oxygen saturation 94% on room air, afebrile. What
actually moved her was none of those numbers. She looked grey, and the nurse
who saw her looked at her and moved her to a resuscitation bay before the
second set of observations was taken.

The ECG showed a narrow complex, irregularly irregular rhythm with no
discernible P waves — atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response.
Bloods came back with a potassium of 3.1 mmol/L and a magnesium of
0.62 mmol/L. Thyroid function was normal. Troponin was 46, rising to 51.

The cardiology registrar asked for metoprolol 2.5 mg intravenously. The
senior nurse disagreed, and said the blood pressure made cardioversion the
right first move. The metoprolol was given. The blood pressure fell to 72/40.

The team then moved to synchronised cardioversion at 150 J. She converted to
sinus rhythm at 78 and her blood pressure recovered. Her electrolytes were
replaced. She went home on day two.

The history that emerged afterwards was that she had been taking amiodarone
prescribed by her general practitioner and had stopped it about three weeks
earlier.

**Teaching point.** Haemodynamic instability changes the algorithm, and the
nurse in the room is often the person best placed to say so. Rate control in
a patient who is already hypotensive is a decision, not a default. The
disagreement in this case was the thing that went right.

## The paper

**Joglar JA, Chung MK, Armbruster AL, et al. 2023 ACC/AHA/ACCP/HRS Guideline
for the Diagnosis and Management of Atrial Fibrillation. Circulation.
2024;149(1):e1–e156.** doi:10.1161/CIR.0000000000001193 — free full text via
PubMed Central.

Most fast AF is a rate problem, and most of the time the pathway is a
rate-controlling drug and a look for the trigger. The guideline's value at
the bedside is that it makes explicit where that pathway stops: when the
patient is not tolerating the rhythm — hypotensive, poorly perfused,
ischaemic, in pulmonary oedema — the question is no longer which drug but how
soon the rhythm can be restored. For the nurse, that matters because the
finding which moves a patient across that line is usually a bedside one, and
it is usually available before the next observation set. Knowing the branch
exists is what turns "her BP is 88 over 54" from an observation into an
argument.

Also worth reading:

- Pluymaekers NAHA, Dudink EAMP, Luermans JGLM, et al. Early or Delayed
  Cardioversion in Recent-Onset Atrial Fibrillation. N Engl J Med.
  2019;380(16):1499–1508. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1900353 — the trial behind "wait
  and see", and worth reading precisely because it enrolled only
  haemodynamically stable patients. Paywalled.
- Brieger D, Amerena J, Attia J, et al. National Heart Foundation of
  Australia and the Cardiac Society of Australia and New Zealand: Australian
  Clinical Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Atrial Fibrillation
  2018. Heart Lung Circ. 2018;27(10):1209–1266.
  doi:10.1016/j.hlc.2018.06.1043 — the Australian document. Paywalled.

## Drug note — intravenous metoprolol

A cardioselective beta blocker, used intravenously in fast AF to slow
conduction through the AV node. Typically given in small incremental doses
with the rhythm and blood pressure watched between each, because the effect
on rate and the effect on contractility arrive together.

What it does to the patient is the part worth holding onto. Slowing the
ventricular rate lengthens diastole and usually improves filling, which is
why it works. In a patient who is already hypotensive, the negative inotropic
effect can arrive first, and the blood pressure that was being maintained by
the rate goes with it. That is what happened here.

Watch the blood pressure continuously, not at intervals, from the moment it
is drawn up. Watch for bradycardia as the rhythm breaks. Know where the
fluid, the atropine and the defibrillator are before the first dose, not
after it. And if the patient is already compromised before you give it, say
so out loud.
